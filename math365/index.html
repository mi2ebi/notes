ch1 matrices and systems of linear equations

1.1 intro to matrices

linear equation
in \(n\) unknowns, an equaiton (that can be written) in the form \[a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ = b\] where \(aᵢ\) are the given coefficients, \(xᵢ\) are the unknowns, \(b\) is the constant
system of linear equations (also linear system)
a set of linear equations. an \((m × n)\) system has \(m\) linear equations and \(n\) unknowns \[ \left\{ \begin{align*} a₁₁x₁ + a₁₂x₂ + … + a₁ₙxₙ &= b₁ \\ a₂₁x₁ + a₂₂x₂ + … + a₂ₙxₙ &= b₂ \\ …& \\ aₘ₁x₁ + aₘ₂x₂ + … + aₘₙxₙ &= bₘ \\ \end{align*} \right. \]

ex. find two different solutions of \(3x₁ + ex₂ = 10\) and describe the solution set

solving for \(x₁\) gives us \[x₁ = \frac{10}3 - \frac e3 x₂\]

for \(x₂ = 0\) we get \(x₁ = \frac{10}3\)

for \(x₂ = \frac{10}e\) we get \(x₁ = 0\)

the solution set is a line! with slope \(-\frac e3\) and “\(x₂\)-intercept” \(\frac{10}3\)

given a \((2 × 2)\) linear system of equations there are three possibilities:

  • there's exactly 1 solution
  • there aren't any solutions (parallel)
  • there are infinitely many solutions (exact same line)

a system is consistent if it has at least one solution and inconsistent if not

our goal is to write a system as something like \(ax = b\)

matrix
an \((m × n)\) matrix is a rectangular array of the form \[ \begin{bmatrix} a₁₁ & a₁₂ & … & a₁ₙ\\ a₂₁ & a₂₂ & … & a₂ₙ\\ …\\ aₘ₁ & aₘ₂ & … & aₘₙ\\ \end{bmatrix} \] i.e. with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, where the \(aᵢⱼ\) are real numbers

ex. \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 7 & 3\\ 5 & 6 & 4\end{bmatrix}\) is a \((2 × 3)\) matrix

ex. display the \((2 × 3)\) matrix with coefficients \(a₁₁ = 6\), \(a₁₂ = 3\), \(a₁₃ = 7\), \(a₂₁ = 0\), \(a₂₂ = 2\), \(a₂₃ = 4\) \[𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}6 & 3 & 7\\ 0 & 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}\]

we can use matrices to represent linear systems!

consider the system \[ \left\{ \begin{align*} x₁ + 3x₂ &= 6\\ 2x₁ - x₂ &= 5 \end{align*} \right. \]

let's just keep the coefficients and the constants

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 3 & 6\\ 2 & -1 & 5 \end{array} \right] \]

the matrix just on the left of the line is the coefficient matrix. together with the other side it's an augmented matrix

we want to rewrite the matrices so it's easy for us to solve. we'll need some row operations to use on augmented matrices

  1. \(Rᵢ ↔ Rⱼ\) interchange two rows
  2. \(kRᵢ\) multiply a row by a nonzero scalar (real number)
  3. \(Rᵢ + kRⱼ\) add a constant multiple of row \(j\) to row \(i\)

1.2 echelon form & gauss-jordan elimination

an \((m × n)\) matrix is in echelon form if

  1. all rows consisting entirely of zeros are grouped together at the bottom of the matrix
  2. if every nonzero row, the leftmost nonzero entry is 1
  3. the leading entry (i.e. leftmost nonzero entry) of every nonzero row is to the right of the leading entry of every row above

tl;dr “it looks like a staircase”

there are multiple ways to get matrices into echelon form, and at-least-sometimes multiple echelon forms for a given matrix

a matrix is in reduced echelon form if it's in echelon form and each column contiaining a leading 1 has zeros in all entries above that 1. eg \[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 5\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right] \] corresponds to the consistent linear system \[ \left\{ \begin{align*} x₁ + 2x₂ &= 5\\ x₃ &= 0\\ 0 &= 0 \end{align*} \right. \]

now a system is inconsistent iff in reduced echelon form it has a row that looks like \( \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} … & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] \)

ex. we can get \[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 6 & 2 & 1\\ 2 & 0 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \] to reduced echelon form by doing

  • \(R₁ ↔ R₂\)
  • \(R₂ ↔ R₃\)
  • \(\frac12 R₁\)
  • \(\frac16 R₂\)
  • \(R₂ - \frac13 R₃\)
  • \(R₁ - \frac12 R₃\)
which produces \[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 1/2\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1/2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

if we treat this as an augmented matrix for a linear system, it's super easy to find the solution! \(x₁ = \frac12\), \(x₂ = -\frac12\), \(x₃ = 2\)

1.3 consistent linear systems

here our goal is to find out as much information about the system as possible without actually solving it

if \(m < n\) the linear system either is inconsistent or has infinitely many solutions

homogeneous linear system
a linear system where all the \(b\) values are zero

because \(x₁ = x₂ = ⋯ = xₘ = 0\) is always a solution to that, homogeneous linear systems are always consistent

1.5 matrix operations

two matrices \(𝐀\) and \(𝐁\) are equal if they have the same size \(m × n\) and all the entries \(aᵢⱼ\) and \(bᵢⱼ\) are the same

the matrix \(𝐀 + 𝐁\) is defined, if they have the same size, as \((𝐀 + 𝐁)ᵢⱼ = aᵢⱼ + bᵢⱼ\)

if \(𝐀\) is a matrix and \(r ∈ ℝ\), the matrix \(r𝐀\) is defined as \((r𝐀)ᵢⱼ = raᵢⱼ\)

vectors in \(ℝⁿ\)

a vector in \(ℝⁿ\) is of the form \[ 𝐱 = \begin{bmatrix} x₁\\ x₂\\ ⋮\\ xₙ \end{bmatrix} \] where \(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ\) are scalars

a vector \(𝐱 ∈ ℝⁿ\) is secretly an \((n × 1)\) matrix

ex. suppose the augmented matrix of a system is \[ \left[ \begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 0 & -1 & -3 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \end{array} \right] \] \(x₁\) and \(x₂\) are dependent variables (because their columns have leading 1s) and \(x₃\) and \(x₄\) are independent variables.

\[ \left\{ \begin{align*} x₁ - x₃ - 3x₄ &= 0\\ x₂ + 2x₃ + x₄ &= 0 \end{align*} \right. \]

so \[ 𝐱 = \begin{bmatrix} x₁\\ x₂\\ x₃\\ x₄ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x₃ + 3x₄\\ -2x₃ - x₄\\ x₃\\ x₄ \end{bmatrix} = x₃\begin{bmatrix} 1\\ -2\\ 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x₄\begin{bmatrix} 3\\ -1\\ 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

matrix multiplication

let \(𝐱 ∈ ℝⁿ\) and \(𝐀\) be an \(m × n\) matrix. we define the product \(𝐀𝐱\) to be the vector \[ 𝐀𝐱 = \begin{bmatrix} ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ a₁ᵢxᵢ\\ ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ a₂ᵢxᵢ\\ ⋮\\ ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ aₘᵢxᵢ\\ \end{bmatrix} \]

i.e. we take each row of \(𝐀\), multiply each entry in the row by the corresponding entry of \(𝐱\), add them and put them back in the row

for a matrix \(𝐀\) of size \(m × n\) and \(𝐁\) of size \(n × p\), \(𝐀𝐁\) is an \(m × p\) matrix with entries \[ (𝐀𝐁)ᵢⱼ = ∑ₖ₌₁ⁿ aᵢₖbₖⱼ \] (3b1b essence of linalg video)

ex. let \( 𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3\\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \), \( 𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 2 & 1\\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \), then \[ 𝐀𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix} 1\mathord·1 + 2\mathord·2 + 3\mathord·3 & 1\mathord·1 + 2\mathord·1 + 3\mathord·1\\ 1\mathord·1 + 1\mathord·2 + 3\mathord·3 & 1\mathord·1 + 1\mathord·1 + 3\mathord·1\\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 & 6\\ 12 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \] and \[ 𝐁𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix} 1\mathord·1 + 1\mathord·1 & 1\mathord·2 + 1\mathord·1 & 1\mathord·3 + 1\mathord·3\\ 2\mathord·1 + 1\mathord·1 & 2\mathord·2 + 1\mathord·1 & 2\mathord·3 + 1\mathord·3\\ 3\mathord·1 + 1\mathord·1 & 3\mathord·2 + 1\mathord·1 & 3\mathord·3 + 1\mathord·3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 6\\ 3 & 5 & 9\\ 4 & 7 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]

matrix multiplication isn't commutative but it is associative, i.e. \(𝐀(𝐁𝐂) = (𝐀𝐁)𝐂 ≕ 𝐀𝐁𝐂\)

other representations of matrices

we can write an \(m × n\) matrix \(𝐀\) as a row vector of its column vectors \[𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix} 𝐀₁ & 𝐀₂ & … & 𝐀ₙ \end{bmatrix}\]

then we can define \(𝐀𝐱\) as \[𝐀𝐱 = x₁𝐀₁ + x₂𝐀₂ + ⋯ + xₙ𝐀ₙ\]

if \(𝐀\) is an \(m × n\) matrix and \(𝐁\) is an \(n × p\) matrix, we can write matrix multiplication as \[ 𝐀𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix} 𝐀𝐁₁ & 𝐀𝐁₂ & … & 𝐀𝐁ₙ \end{bmatrix} \]

1.6 algebraic properties of matrix operations

matrix addition is commutative and associative because we're just adding the entries which are all real numbers

let \(𝟎\) be the \(m × n\) matrix whose entries are all zero. then \[𝐀 + 𝟎 = 𝟎 + 𝐀 = 𝐀\]

there is a unique \(m × n\) matrix \(𝐏\) such that \(𝐀 + 𝐏 = 𝟎\); specifically \(𝐏ᵢⱼ = -𝐀ᵢⱼ\). this is called the opposite/negative matrix

matrix multiplication is also distributive i.e. \((𝐀 + 𝐁)𝐌 = 𝐀𝐌 + 𝐁𝐌\)

for scalar multiplication:

  • \((r + s)𝐀 = r𝐀 + s𝐀\)
  • \((rs)𝐀 = r(s𝐀) = s(r𝐀) = rs𝐀\)
  • \(r(𝐀 + 𝐁) = r𝐀 + r𝐁\)

transpose matrix

if \(𝐀\) is an \(m × n\) matrix, \(𝐀^⊤\) is an \(n × m\) matrix with entries \((𝐀^⊤)ᵢⱼ = aⱼᵢ\)

\(𝐀\) is symmetric if \(𝐀 = 𝐀^⊤\)

properties of transpose:

  • \((𝐀 + 𝐁)^⊤ = 𝐀^⊤ + 𝐁^⊤\)
  • \((𝐀𝐁)^⊤ = 𝐁^⊤ + 𝐀^⊤\)
  • \({𝐀^⊤}^⊤ = 𝐀\)

identity matrix

the \(n × n\) matrix \(𝐈ₙ\) is the zero matrix but with 1s in the diagonal, eg \[ 𝐈₃ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

if \(𝐀\) is an \(m × n\) matrix, \(𝐈ₘ 𝐀 = 𝐀𝐈ₙ = 𝐀\)

1.7 linear independence and nonsingular matrices

linear independence

let \(𝐀₁ = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(𝐀₂ = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\)

can we find \(x₁, x₂ ∈ ℝ\) such that \(x₁𝐀₁ + x₂𝐀 ₂ = 𝟎\) ?

i.e. \( x₁ \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + x₂ \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 𝟎 \)

i.e. \[ \left\{ \begin{align*} x₁ + 2x₂ &= 0\\ 2x₁ + x₂ &= 0 \end{align*} \right. \] which is a homogeneous system!

in reduced echelon form this is \[ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{array} \right] \] so the only solution is \(x₁ = x₂ = 0\)

a set of vectors \(𝐀₁, 𝐀₂, …, 𝐀ₙ ∈ ℝᵐ\) is linearly independent if the only solution to the equation \[x₁𝐀₁ + x₂𝐀₂ + ⋯ + xₙ𝐀ₙ = 𝟎\] is \[x₁ = x₂ = ⋯ = xₘ = 0\]

otherwise they're linearly dependent

unit vectors

a unit vector is a vector with length 1. the unit vectors in \(ℝⁿ\) are \[ 𝐞₁ = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ ⋮\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, 𝐞₂ = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ ⋮\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, … 𝐞ₙ = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ ⋮\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

the set \(\set{𝐞₁, 𝐞₂, …, 𝐞ₙ}\) is linearly independent

any vector \(𝐱\) can be written as \(x₁𝐞₁ + x₂𝐞₂ + ⋯ + xₙ𝐞ₙ\)

nonsingular matrices

an \(n × n\) matrix is nonsingular if its column vectors are linearly independent. otherwise it's singular

if you can RREF it into being an identity matrix, it's nonsingular

if \(𝐀\) is a square matrix and \(𝐛 ∈ ℝⁿ\), \(𝐀\) is nonsingular if \[𝐀𝐱 = 𝐛\] has exactly one solution

if \(𝐀\) is nonsingular, so is \(𝐀^⊤\). this means \(𝐀\) is also nonsingular if its row vectors are linearly independent

1.9 matrix inverses and their properties

we can solve \(ax = b\) by doing \(x = a⁻¹b\). can we do the same for matrices?

a square nonsingular matrix \(𝐀\) is invertible if we can find another square matrix \(𝐏 ≕ 𝐀⁻¹\) such that \[𝐀⁻¹𝐀 = 𝐀𝐀⁻¹ = 𝐈\]

all nonsingular matrices are invertible and vice versa. they mean the same thing

ex. invert \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3\\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

1. augment with \(2 × 2\) identity matrix

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] \]

2. find its RREF

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & -\frac15 & \frac35\\ 0 & 1 & \frac25 & -\frac15 \end{array} \right] \]

once the identity matrix is on the left, the right is the inverse matrix

now if we want to solve \(𝐀𝐱 = 𝐛\), we just multiply both sides on the left by \(𝐀⁻¹\) to get \[𝐱 = 𝐀⁻¹𝐁\]

if \(𝐀\) and \(𝐁\) are invertible \(n × n\) matrices,

  • \((𝐀⁻¹)⁻¹ = 𝐀\)
  • \((𝐀𝐁)⁻¹ = 𝐁⁻¹𝐀⁻¹\)
  • \(𝐀^⊤\) is invertible and \((𝐀^⊤)⁻¹ = (𝐀⁻¹)^⊤\)
  • \((r𝐀)⁻¹ = \frac1r 𝐀⁻¹\)

determinant of \(2 × 2\) matrix

let \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\). the determinant of \(𝐀\) is defined as \(Δ = ad - bc\)

if \(Δ = 0\), \(𝐀\) isn't invertible. if \(Δ ≠ 0\), \(𝐀\) is invertible and \[ 𝐀⁻¹ = \frac1Δ \begin{bmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a\\ \end{bmatrix} \]

ch2 vectors in 2-space and 3-space

‘2-space’ just means \(ℝ²\) and ‘3-space’ just means \(ℝ³\)

2.1 vectors in the plane

physical vectors are used to characterize stuff that has both a magnitude/length and a direction. a geometric vector is a directed line segment. when we just care about the components we call it an algebraic vector

geometric vectors

suppose \(A = (a₁, a₂)\) and \(B = (b₁, b₂)\) are points. the directed line segment from \(A\) to \(B\) is called \(\overrightarrow{AB}\). \(A\) is the initial point and \(B\) is the terminal point

the magnitude of the vector, \(\Vert\overrightarrow{AB}\Vert\), is \(\sqrt{(b₂ - a₂)² + (b₁ - a₁)²}\)

two geometric vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction

the position vector of a certain vector \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) is the vector \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) that equals \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) but starts at the origin. \(P = (b₁ - a₁, b₂ - a₂)\), and the components of both vectors are \(⟨ b₁ - a₁, b₂ - a₂⟩\)

algebraic vectors, operations on vectors

let \(𝐯\) be a geometric vector with position vector \(\overrightarrow{OP}\), where \(P = (v₁, v₂)\). then the algebraic vector \(𝐯\) is the column vector \(\begin{bmatrix}v₁\\ v₂\end{bmatrix}\)

we can add and subtract vectors just like we add matrices. \[ 𝐮 + 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}u₁ + v₁\\ u₂ + v₂\end{bmatrix} \] \[ 𝐮 - 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}u₁ - v₁\\ u₂ - v₂\end{bmatrix} \]

geometrically we move the initial point of \(𝐯\) to the terminal point of \(𝐮\)

unit vectors in the plane

in \(ℝ²\) the unit vectors are \(𝐢 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(𝐣 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\)

we can represent any vector \(𝐯\) as \(v₁𝐢 + v₂𝐣\)

we can make an arbitrary unit vector \(\^𝐯\) from a vector \(𝐯\) by \(\^𝐯 = \frac{𝐯}{\Vert𝐯\Vert}\)

2.2 vectors in space

we have three orthogonal axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\). positive \(z\) is up (right-hand rule) as opposed to down (left-hand rule)

the plane containing the \(x\) and \(y\) axes is called the \(xy\)-plane etc

given two points \(P = (p₁, p₂, p₃)\) and \(Q = (q₁, q₂, q₃)\), the distance between them is \[ \sqrt{(q₁ - p₁)² + (q₂ - p₂)² + (q₃ + p₃)²} \]

unit vectors in space

here we have \(𝐢 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐣 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐤 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 0\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\)

2.3 dot product and cross product

dot product

the dot product of two vectors is \[ 𝐮 ⋅ 𝐯 = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + ⋯ + uₙvₙ \]

ex. \( 𝐮 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}, 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}2\\ 9\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \)

\[ 𝐮 ⋅ 𝐯 = 1 ⋅ 2 + 3 ⋅ 9 + (-1) ⋅ 1 = 28 \]

angle between two vectors

let \(𝐮, 𝐯\) be two nonzero vectors and \(θ\) be the angle between them. then \[ 𝐮 ⋅ 𝐯 = \Vert𝐮\Vert \Vert𝐯\Vert \cosθ \]

two vectors are orthogonal iff their dot product is zero

properties of the dot product

  • dot product is commutative and distributive
  • \(𝐮 ⋅ 𝐮 = \Vert𝐮\Vert² ≥ 0\)
  • \((c𝐮) ⋅ 𝐯 = c(𝐮 ⋅ 𝐯)\)

projection

\( \gdef\comp{\operatorname{comp}} \gdef\proj{\operatorname{proj}} \)

the component of \(𝐯\) in the direction of \(𝐮\) is \(\comp_𝐮(𝐯) = \frac{𝐮 ⋅ 𝐯}{\Vert𝐮\Vert}\)

the projection of \(𝐯\) in the direction of \(𝐮\) is the vector \(\proj_𝐮(𝐯) = \comp_𝐮(𝐯) \^𝐮\)

cross product

suppose we want to find a vector in 3-space orthogonal to both \(𝐮\) and \(𝐯\).

their cross product is \[ 𝐮 × 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}u₂v₃ - u₃v₂\\ u₃v₁ - u₁v₃\\ u₁v₂ - u₂v₁\end{bmatrix} \]
