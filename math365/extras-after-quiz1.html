1.

Let \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐂 = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\).

Compute \(𝐀 + 2𝐁\), \(𝐀𝐁 - 𝐁𝐀\), and \((𝐀 + 𝐁)𝐂\).

Solution

\( 𝐀 + 2𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0 & -2\\ 4 & 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 7 & 6\end{bmatrix} \)

\(𝐀𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1\\ 8 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐁𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}-3 & -2\\ 5 & 8\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐀𝐁 - 𝐁𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}7 & 3\\ 3 & -7\end{bmatrix}\)

\( (𝐀 + 𝐁)𝐂 = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 5 & 5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 1 & 3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3\\ 15 & 15\end{bmatrix} \)

2.

Let \(𝐀\) be a \(3 × 2\) matrix and \(𝐁\) be a \(2 × 4\) matrix. What are the sizes of \(𝐀^⊤\), \(𝐁^⊤\), \(𝐀𝐁\), and \(𝐁^⊤𝐀^⊤\)?

Solution

\(𝐀^⊤\) is \(3 × 2\), \(𝐁^⊤\) is \(4 × 2\), \(𝐀𝐁\) is \(3 × 4\), and \(𝐁^⊤𝐀^⊤ = (𝐀𝐁)^⊤\) is \(4 × 3\).

3.

Find all \(2 × 2\) matrices \(𝐀\) such that \(𝐀^⊤ = -𝐀\). Such matrices are called skew-symmetric.

Solution

Let \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{bmatrix}\). Then \[ 𝐀^⊤ = \begin{bmatrix}a & c\\ b & d\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-a & -b\\ -c & -d\end{bmatrix} = -𝐀 \]

\(a = -a\) and \(d = -d\) so those must both be zero, and \(c = -b\). So all skew-symmetric \(2 × 2\) matrices have the form \[ 𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}0 & r\\ -r & 0\end{bmatrix},~~r ∈ ℝ \]

4.

For each set of vectors, are they linearly independent?

(a) \( 𝐮 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 2\end{bmatrix}, 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}3\\ 6\end{bmatrix} \)

(b) \( 𝐮 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\\ 2\end{bmatrix}, 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\\ 1\end{bmatrix}, 𝐰 = \begin{bmatrix}2\\ -1\\ 3\end{bmatrix} \)

(c) \( 𝐮 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}, 𝐯 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\\ 1\end{bmatrix}, 𝐰 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \)

Solution

(a) Dependent: \(𝐯 = 3𝐮\).

(b) The corresponding matrix can be RREFd into \(\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 2\\ 0 & 1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\), which has 2 pivots (leading 1s), but we have 3 vectors, so they're linearly dependent. Specifically, \(𝐰 = 2𝐮 - 𝐯\).

(c) This can be RREFd into \(\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\). As there are 3 pivots, the vectors are linearly independent.

5.

Is \(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\\ 0 & 1 & 4\\ 5 & 6 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) nonsingular?

Solution

The RREF is \(𝐈₃\), the \(3 × 3\) identity matrix, so \(𝐀\) is nonsingular.

6.

True or false: If \(𝐀\) is a square matrix and its rows are linearly independent, then \(𝐀\) is nonsingular. Briefly justify.

Solution

True! For a square matrix, linear independence of the rows is equivalent to linear indepence of the columns, which is the definition of nonsingularity. Equivalently, if the rows are independent, RREF has a pivot in every row, and hence in every column (it's a square matrix), which makes it an identity matrix.

Invert each matrix, if possible.

\(𝐀 = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 7\\ 2 & 6\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐁 = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 4 & 2\end{bmatrix}\), \(𝐂 = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & -1\\ 2 & 5 & 0\\ -1 & -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\)

Solution

The determinant of \(𝐀\) (which later we'll write as \(\det 𝐀\)) is \(4(6) - 7(2) = 10 ≠ 0\), so it's invertible. \[ 𝐀⁻¹ = \frac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}6 & -7\\ -2 & 4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.6 & -0.7\\ -0.2 & 0.4\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\det 𝐁 = 2(2) - 1(4) = 0\), so we can't invert it.

Augment \(𝐂\) with \(𝐈₃\) and row reduce:

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & -1 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ 2 & 5 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ -1 & -1 & 2 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{array} \right] \]

After (1) \(R₂ - 2R₁\) (2) \(R₃ + R₁\) (3) \(R₃ - R₂\) (4) \(-R₃\) (5) \(R₂ - 2R₃\) (6) \(R₁ + R₃\) (7) \(R₁ - 2R₂\), we get \[ 𝐂⁻¹ = \begin{bmatrix}-10 & 3 & -5\\ 4 & -1 & 2\\ -3 & 1 & -1\end{bmatrix} \]

Use \(𝐀⁻¹\) from the previous problem to solve \(𝐀𝐱 = 𝐛 = \begin{bmatrix}5\\ 4\end{bmatrix}\).

Solution

\( 𝐱 = 𝐀⁻¹𝐛 = \frac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}6 & -7\\ -2 & 4\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}5\\ 4\end{bmatrix} = \frac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}30 - 28\\ -10 + 16\end{bmatrix} = \frac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}2\\ 6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.2\\ 0.6\end{bmatrix} \)

9.

Let \(𝐀\) be an \(n × n\) invertible matrix. Show that if \(𝐀𝐱 = 𝟎\) (the zero vector), then \(𝐱 = 𝟎\).

Solution

Multiply both sides of \(𝐀𝐱 = 𝟎\) on the left by \(𝐀⁻¹\): \[ \begin{align*} 𝐀⁻¹(𝐀𝐱) &= 𝐀⁻¹𝟎\\ (𝐀⁻¹𝐀)𝐱 &= 𝟎\\ 𝐈ₙ𝐱 &= 𝟎\\ 𝐱 &= 𝟎 \end{align*} \]

Since the only solution is the trivial one, the columns of \(𝐀\) are linearly independent, so \(𝐀\) is nonsingular (which we already knew, since it's invertible).
